# Validation

> **TL;DR.** Validation is communicating that someone's feelings *make sense* given their perspective —
> "of course you're upset, anyone would be." You can validate without agreeing, and it's one of the
> fastest ways to de-escalate tension and make someone feel genuinely understood.

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** high · **Updated:** 2026-06-04

## What it is

From Marsha Linehan's DBT, **validation** is acknowledging that a person's emotional reaction is
*understandable* — not necessarily *correct* or *agreed with*, just legitimate given how they see things:
"Given what you thought was happening, it makes total sense you'd feel betrayed."

The move people miss: **you can validate the feeling while disagreeing with the conclusion.** "I get why
that landed as an insult — *and* I didn't mean it that way." Invalidation — "you're overreacting," "don't
feel that way," or jumping straight to fixing it — does the opposite: it tells someone their inner
experience is wrong, and they dig in. Validation lowers the temperature so the real conversation can
happen.

## How to apply

- Before you problem-solve, disagree, or reassure, **validate first**: "That sounds really frustrating."
  Most people escalate because they don't feel heard, not because of the issue itself.
- Validate the **emotion**, not necessarily the *behavior* or the *facts* — "it makes sense you're angry"
  is not "you were right to yell."
- Kill the reflex fixes — "at least…", "you should…", "calm down" — they all read as invalidation.
