# Lead with warmth

> **TL;DR.** People size you up on two axes — warmth (can I trust you?) and competence (can I respect
> you?) — and they judge **warmth first**. Leading with competence to impress backfires; project warmth
> and trustworthiness first, and your competence becomes an asset instead of a threat.

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** high · **Updated:** 2026-06-04

## What it is

Decades of social-cognition research (Fiske, Cuddy & Glick) show that **warmth and competence** are the
two universal dimensions on which we judge others — and warmth is assessed **first and weighted more
heavily**, because "friend or foe?" historically mattered before "are they capable?"

Amy Cuddy's applied point (*Presence*): people who try to establish competence *before* warmth read as
cold and even untrustworthy — impressive but off-putting. Lead with warmth and the same competence reads
as *generous* rather than *threatening*. Trust is the gateway; respect and influence follow it.

## How to apply

- In a first meeting, **establish warmth before you demonstrate competence**: be present, genuinely
  curious, a little human — *then* let your skill show.
- Small warmth signals do a lot: real eye contact, using their name, remembering a detail, a genuine
  smile, validating their view before adding yours.
- If you tend to "lead with the résumé," consciously hold it. Competence without warmth doesn't make
  people like you — it makes them wary.
