# Intent vs. impact

> **TL;DR.** We judge *ourselves* by our intentions but *others* by their impact — and we wrongly read
> intent *from* impact ("you hurt me, so you *meant* to"). Separating the two defuses most spirals: name the
> impact, get curious about the intent, and own your impact even when your intent was good.

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** high · **Updated:** 2026-06-04

## What it is

From the Harvard Negotiation Project's *Difficult Conversations*. A core error in conflict: someone does
something that **lands badly** (impact), and we instantly assume they **meant** it (intent) — "he didn't
text back, he doesn't care." But intent is invisible; we're *inventing* it, usually in the least charitable
way. Meanwhile we expect others to excuse *our* bad impacts because we know our intent was fine.

Two fixes:

- When *you're* hurt: separate them — "when X happened, I felt Y" (impact) — then **ask** about intent rather
  than assuming it. "Move from certainty to curiosity."
- When *you* caused hurt: **own the impact** even if your intent was innocent. "I didn't mean to — *and* I
  can see it landed badly. I'm sorry." Don't hide behind "but I didn't mean to."

## How to apply

- Catch yourself building a villain story from a bad impact. The honest version is "that *landed* badly,"
  not "they *did* that to me" — then check it out loud.
- "I didn't mean to" is the *start* of a sentence, not the end — pair it with acknowledging the impact.
