# The halo effect ("what is beautiful is good")

> **TL;DR.** People unconsciously assume attractive people are also more competent, trustworthy, and
> sociable. It's a real, measured bias — looks leak into judgments that have nothing to do with looks.
> Worth knowing both to use it and to distrust it.

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** high · **Updated:** 2026-06-04

## What it is

The classic finding (Dion, Berscheid & Walster, 1972, "What Is Beautiful Is Good"): shown only a photo,
people rate attractive individuals as having more desirable personalities and better life prospects. A
meta-analysis (Eagly et al., 1991) confirmed a robust **physical-attractiveness stereotype** — strongest
for social competence, weaker for honesty, but real across the board.

It's a **halo**: one salient positive trait (looks) spills into unrelated judgments (competence, trust).
It runs fast and below awareness — in hiring, dating, courtrooms, classrooms. It isn't fair; it's just how
perception works, and pretending it doesn't exist doesn't help you.

## How to apply

- **Use it where it's cheap and legitimate:** first impressions (interviews, dates, photos) run on this
  bias, so the controllable levers — [grooming](grooming.md), [fit](style-and-fit.md), a genuine
  [smile](../body-language/the-genuine-smile.md) — pay off out of proportion to their effort.
- **Distrust it in yourself:** notice when you credit someone competence or character on the basis of
  looks — or discredit yourself the same way. The halo is a bias to correct for, not a truth.
