# Disruption & its critics

> **TL;DR.** Clayton Christensen's **disruptive innovation** explains how small entrants topple incumbents *from below* — a genuinely useful theory that became a Silicon Valley buzzword. The pushback: "disruption" is **over-applied and over-claimed** (Lepore), and Thiel argues founders should aim to **create and monopolize**, not obsess over "disrupting" incumbents.

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** contested · **Updated:** 2026-06-06

## What it is

**The theory (Christensen, *The Innovator's Dilemma*):** incumbents listen to their best customers and chase higher margins, *rationally* ignoring cheap, low-end, "worse" products. Those entrants improve until they take the market from below. Well-run firms fail *because* they did the textbook-correct thing — the genuine, durable insight.

!!! warning "Contested — a real theory, but over-stretched"
    - **Jill Lepore's critique** ("The Disruption Machine," 2014): the theory is applied retroactively and loosely, its original case studies are cherry-picked, and "disruption" became a slogan that explains everything and predicts little.
    - **Thiel's angle:** "disruption" frames you as a scrappy attacker *fighting* incumbents — i.e., *competing*. Better to **create something new and own it** than to define yourself by whom you're disrupting.
    Useful residue: disruption names a *real* failure mode of incumbents, but it's a lens, not a law — and "we're disrupting X" is not a strategy.

## How to apply

- **Use disruption diagnostically** ("is a cheaper, good-enough entrant creeping up on this incumbent?"), not as a pitch.
- If you're the **incumbent**, guard the low end you're tempted to cede; if you're the **entrant**, prefer *creating a new market* to *attacking a strong one*.
