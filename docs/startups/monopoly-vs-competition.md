# Monopoly vs. competition

> **TL;DR.** Thiel's central, deliberately provocative claim: **"competition is for losers."** Perfect competition drives profits to zero, so a startup's goal is to **escape competition and become a monopoly** — so good at one thing that no one offers a close substitute. Competition is good for *society*; it's bad for *you*.

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** high · **Updated:** 2026-06-06

## What it is

Econ 101 prizes competition; Thiel inverts it for the founder. Under perfect competition no firm earns economic profit — you survive at the margin. A **monopoly** (the "create something new and own it" kind) is what funds a long view and real innovation. Hence: *"All happy companies are different — each earns a monopoly by solving a unique problem. All unhappy companies are alike — they failed to escape competition."*

A tell: both types **lie** about it — monopolists frame their market *broadly* to look small (avoid scrutiny); competitors frame it *narrowly* to look unique.

**Four sources of durable monopoly:**

1. **Proprietary technology** — ~**10× better** at something that matters (not 2×).
2. **Network effects** — more valuable as more people use it (see [network effects](network-effects-and-increasing-returns.md)).
3. **Economies of scale** — margins improve as you grow (software's marginal cost ≈ 0).
4. **Branding** — a strong, hard-to-copy brand.

And aim to be the **last mover**, not the first: make the *final* great development in a market and collect monopoly profits for years.

## How to apply

- **Ask "what's the real market, and what share would I have?"** Owning ~100% of something tiny beats a sliver of something huge ([start small](start-small-and-monopolize.md)).
- **Chase 10×, not 10%.** Incrementally-better products don't escape competition.
- Don't romanticize "crushing rivals" — fighting in a red ocean *is* the trap.
