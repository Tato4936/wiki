# Moats

> **TL;DR.** "Moat" is the strategy/investing word for Thiel's **monopoly**: a *durable* structural advantage that protects profits from competition. Porter formalized where moats come from (Five Forces); Buffett made "economic moat" the lens for picking businesses that compound for decades.

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** high · **Updated:** 2026-06-06

## What it is

- **Porter's Five Forces** (1979): an industry's profitability is set by rivalry, buyer power, supplier power, threat of entrants, and substitutes. A moat is a position that *blunts* these forces, so you keep more of the value you create.
- **Buffett's economic moat:** buy businesses with a *durable* advantage (brand, switching costs, network effects, cost advantage, regulatory/IP) and a long runway — the moat keeps the castle earning.

These map almost one-to-one onto Thiel's four monopoly sources (proprietary tech, network effects, scale, brand). Shared insight: **profits come from defensibility, not from being good once.** A product anyone can copy competes its margins away.

Modern deepening: Hamilton Helmer's **7 Powers** (scale economies, network economies, counter-positioning, switching costs, branding, cornered resource, process power) — the sharpest current taxonomy of moats *(backlog)*.

## How to apply

- **Ask "what's the moat?" of any business** (including your own): *why can't a well-funded rival copy this in two years?* No good answer ⇒ the advantage is temporary.
- Distinguish a **better product** (transient) from a **structural moat** (durable), and build toward the latter.
