# Personal space (proxemics)

> **TL;DR.** How close you stand is its own message. Edward Hall mapped the zones we keep — intimate,
> personal, social, public — and stepping into the wrong one (too close, too soon) feels like an intrusion,
> while staying too far reads as cold. The "right" distance is also culture-dependent.

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** high · **Updated:** 2026-06-04

## What it is

Anthropologist Edward Hall coined **proxemics** — the study of interpersonal distance. He described four
rough zones (Western norms):

- **Intimate** (~0–18 in) — partners, close family; intrusion here by others feels threatening.
- **Personal** (~1.5–4 ft) — friends and good acquaintances; normal conversation.
- **Social** (~4–12 ft) — acquaintances, business.
- **Public** (~12 ft+) — addressing a group.

Distance signals the relationship: moving closer claims intimacy, backing off claims distance. Standing too
close reliably creates discomfort. Zones vary a lot by **culture** (and context — a packed elevator
suspends the rules).

## How to apply

- **Respect the zone for the relationship.** Don't crowd into someone's personal space before the
  relationship warrants it — it registers as pushy even if your words are friendly.
- Use distance as a **read**: if someone subtly backs up or angles away, you're too close; if they close
  the gap, that's a green light.
- Across cultures and in new relationships, **start a little farther and let them set the distance.**
