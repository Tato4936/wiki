# The fluency illusion

> **TL;DR.** Rereading and highlighting *feel* productive because they make text feel familiar and easy — but that **fluency is mistaken for mastery.** They're among the lowest-utility study methods. The fix: test yourself, because retrieval gives an honest read on what you actually know.

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** high · **Updated:** 2026-06-04

## What it is

When you reread, the material becomes easy to process, and your brain misreads that **ease as understanding** — the "illusion of competence" (or illusions of knowing). You feel ready; then the blank exam page says otherwise. Dunlosky et al. rated **rereading and highlighting as low utility**, yet surveys (e.g., Karpicke et al., 2009) find they're exactly what most students default to.

This is a **metacognition** problem: our internal gauge of learning is poorly calibrated, biased by fluency. The cure is to replace the felt sense of knowing with **objective evidence** — can you retrieve it cold? (Hence [retrieval practice](retrieval-practice.md) does double duty: it *builds* learning and *measures* it honestly.)

## How to apply

- **Demote rereading and highlighting** to a first pass, not a study method.
- **Calibrate with retrieval:** after studying, close everything and test. If you can't produce it, you haven't learned it — regardless of how familiar it felt.
- **Distrust "this feels easy."** Under [desirable difficulties](desirable-difficulties.md), easy often means *not learning*.
