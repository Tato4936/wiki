# Interleaving

> **TL;DR.** **Mix different problem types or topics within a session** instead of doing all of one kind then all of the next ("blocking"). Interleaving feels harder and slower, but produces better retention and — crucially — the ability to *tell similar things apart* and pick the right approach.

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** high · **Updated:** 2026-06-04

## What it is

In Rohrer & Taylor (2007), students who practiced mixed math problem types ("interleaved") massively outperformed those who practiced them in blocks on a delayed test — despite the blockers looking better *during* practice. Blocking lets you mindlessly reuse the same procedure; interleaving forces you to **first figure out which approach a problem needs**, which is the actual skill on a test or in real life.

It's especially powerful for **discrimination** — categories you need to tell apart (math problem types, bird species, painters' styles, medical cases). It's a [desirable difficulty](desirable-difficulties.md): the constant switching costs short-term smoothness and buys long-term transfer.

!!! note "Interleave *related* things"
    Mix items that are confusable or share a structure (types of integration problems). Randomly mixing unrelated subjects is just task-switching overhead.

## How to apply

- **Shuffle problem sets** so consecutive problems need different methods — don't do 20 of the same in a row.
- **Rotate among a few related topics** in a session rather than one topic per day.
- Expect it to *feel* worse than blocking. That discomfort is the discrimination skill forming.
