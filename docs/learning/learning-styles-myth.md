# Learning styles (myth)

> **TL;DR.** The popular idea that you learn better when teaching is matched to your "style" (visual / auditory / kinesthetic) has **no credible supporting evidence**, despite being believed by most people and teachers. You have *preferences*; matching instruction to them doesn't improve learning. Match the method to the **content** and use **all** modalities.

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** contested · **Updated:** 2026-06-04

## What it is

The "meshing hypothesis" claims each learner has a style and learns best when material is delivered in it. It's intuitive and hugely popular — and not supported.

!!! warning "Contested — the evidence isn't there"
    Pashler et al. (2008) reviewed the field and found almost **no studies using the proper experimental design** (cross over styles × instruction methods and check for an interaction), and the few that did **failed to find** the predicted benefit. People genuinely have *preferences*, but teaching to them doesn't improve outcomes. Belief in learning styles is widespread (often cited ~90% of people), which is why it persists — popularity, not proof.

What *does* matter: matching the modality to the **content** (learn geography with maps, music by ear), and using **multiple** modalities for everyone (see [dual coding](dual-coding.md)).

## How to apply

- **Don't pigeonhole yourself** ("I'm a visual learner") or avoid a modality because it's "not your style."
- **Pick methods by evidence, not style:** [retrieval](retrieval-practice.md), [spacing](spaced-repetition.md), [interleaving](interleaving.md) help everyone.
- **Use words + visuals + doing** together; let the *material* dictate the format.
