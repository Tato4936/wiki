# Desirable difficulties

> **TL;DR.** Conditions that make learning feel **slower and harder** in the moment often make it **stick far better** long-term — and the easy stuff (rereading, cramming) does the reverse. The deep lesson: **performance during study ≠ durable learning.** Don't trust how fluent it feels.

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** high · **Updated:** 2026-06-04

## What it is

Robert Bjork's principle: some difficulties are **desirable** because the extra effort to overcome them strengthens memory and transfer. The catch is that they *depress short-term performance* while *improving long-term retention* — so they feel worse even as they work better.

The key distinction is **learning vs. performance** (Soderstrom & Bjork): how fast you seem to "get it" today (performance) is a poor and often *inverted* signal of how much you'll retain later (learning). Massing and rereading inflate performance and starve learning; spacing, testing, and interleaving do the opposite.

The desirable difficulties are the techniques in this section: **retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, varied practice, and generation.**

!!! note "*Desirable* — not just any difficulty"
    A difficulty only helps if the learner can actually overcome it. Missing prerequisites, bad instruction, or noise are *undesirable* difficulties — they just impair learning. The goal is productive struggle, not struggle for its own sake.

## How to apply

- **Default to the method that feels harder** (closing the book to recall, mixing topics, spacing reviews) — that discomfort is usually the learning happening.
- **Stop using "this feels easy/familiar" as your gauge.** Judge learning by whether you can *retrieve* it cold (see [the fluency illusion](the-fluency-illusion.md)).
