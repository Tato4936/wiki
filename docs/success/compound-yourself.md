# Compound yourself

> **TL;DR.** Altman's first and most important rule: aim for an **exponential**, not linear, career — *"each unit of work you do should generate more and more results."* The lever isn't this year's output; it's the **rate** at which your skills, reputation, capital, and relationships compound. Over a long horizon, a higher rate beats a higher starting level by orders of magnitude.

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** medium · **Updated:** 2026-06-06

## What it is

Altman put "compound yourself" *first* — you want a career that **compounds** rather than adding linearly. Naval echoes it: *"all the returns in life, whether in wealth, relationships, or knowledge, come from compound interest."*

The practical reframe: most people optimize the **level** (a raise, this one project). Compounders optimize the **rate** — choosing roles, skills, and bets that make *next* year's work more productive than this year's. It's the same exponent as [the compounding of capital](../goals/the-compounding-math.md), applied to yourself.

Corollary: **don't grind on something with a low compounding rate.** A prestigious job that builds no durable, leverageable advantage is a slow-growth asset, however shiny.

## How to apply

- **Judge opportunities by their compounding rate**, not their level: "does this make me more capable / connected / leveraged *next* year?"
- **Start young** — learning and reputation compound longest from an early base.
- Periodically **cut low-compounding commitments**, even comfortable ones.
