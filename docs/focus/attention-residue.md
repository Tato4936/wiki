# Attention residue & task-switching

> **TL;DR.** When you switch tasks, part of your attention stays stuck on the previous one — **attention residue** — and degrades performance on the next, especially when the first task was left unfinished or you were rushed. "Multitasking" is really rapid switching, and each switch charges a reconfiguration tax. The fix is to **single-task and close the loop before moving on.**

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** high · **Updated:** 2026-06-08

## What it is

Leroy (2009) coined **attention residue**: cognition lingers on Task A after you move to Task B, so you bring less than your full mind to B. Residue is worse when A is **unfinished** or you switched under **time pressure** — exactly the conditions of a notification-driven workday. Her key remedy: a brief **"ready-to-resume" plan** (jot where you are / next step) before switching measurably reduces the residue, because it closes the open loop the mind keeps chewing on.

Underneath sits the broader **task-switching cost**: the brain must reconfigure its "task set" each time, which takes time and raises errors (Monsell, 2003). So-called multitasking is serial switching paying this tax repeatedly — and heavy media multitaskers were actually *worse* at filtering irrelevant information (Ophir, Nass & Wagner, 2009). The cost is invisible on a clock but shows up as slower, sloppier work.

## How to apply

- **Single-task in blocks.** Batch similar work; don't interleave deep work with "quick checks."
- **Close the loop before you switch:** write the next concrete step so the unfinished task stops haunting the next one (Leroy's ready-to-resume).
- **Kill ambient interruption:** notifications off, one tab/task at a time — the cheapest performance gain available.
- **Batch comms** into windows instead of monitoring continuously.
