# Identity-based habits

> **TL;DR.** Durable change works from the **inside out**: aim at *who you want to be*, not just the outcome you want. "I want to run a marathon" is an outcome goal; "**I'm a runner**" is an identity — and each time you act on it you cast a vote for it. Habits stick when they become evidence for the kind of person you believe you are.

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** medium · **Updated:** 2026-06-08

## What it is

Clear distinguishes three layers of change: **outcomes** (what you get), **processes** (what you do), and **identity** (what you believe about yourself). Most people start at outcomes ("lose 20 lbs"). Clear argues the leverage is at identity: decide the *type of person* who gets that outcome, then let each small action be **"a vote for that identity."** Behavior that conflicts with self-image tends to get abandoned; behavior that *confirms* it compounds.

The mechanism is plausible and has theoretical backing — **self-perception theory** (Bem): we infer who we are partly by observing our own behavior, so repeated actions update the self-concept, which then makes consistent action feel natural rather than effortful.

!!! warning "Practitioner framework, not a clean RCT result"
    The three-layers / "votes for an identity" framing is Clear's synthesis, not a directly tested experimental finding. The underlying ideas (self-perception, value/self-affirmation) have research support, but the specific claim "target identity → better habit adherence" is reasoned, not proven. Use it as a useful lens, with `medium` confidence.

## How to apply

- **State the identity, then the proof:** "I'm someone who trains" → the smallest action that proves it today.
- **Count the votes, not the streak's perfection:** you don't need unanimous, just a majority — see [how habits form](how-habits-form.md).
- **Frame relapses as off-brand**, not as "I failed the goal" — "that's not like me" pulls you back faster than guilt.
