# The habit loop

> **TL;DR.** A habit is a learned **cue → routine → reward** loop that, with repetition, runs automatically — the brain offloads it so the conscious mind can do other things. To change a behavior you rarely fight the loop head-on; you **keep the cue and the reward, and swap the routine**.

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** high · **Updated:** 2026-06-08

## What it is

Duhigg's framing (from the habit-formation research, esp. MIT/Graybiel's work on the basal ganglia): every habit has three parts.

- **Cue** — the trigger that tells the brain to go on autopilot (time, place, emotional state, preceding action, other people).
- **Routine** — the behavior itself.
- **Reward** — what the behavior delivers, which teaches the loop to fire again.

Clear adds a fourth term between cue and routine — **craving** (the *anticipation* of the reward). That's the engine: with repetition the cue starts to *predict* the reward, and the craving pulls the routine out of you before you've decided anything. Neurologically the behavior "chunks" into the basal ganglia and the decision-making cortex goes quiet — which is why habits persist even when the actual reward fades.

This is also why willpower is the wrong tool: by the time you're "deciding," the loop has already fired. The leverage is upstream (the cue) and downstream (the reward), not the routine in the moment.

## How to apply

- **Diagnose the loop before changing it.** For a habit you dislike, name the exact cue and the *real* reward (often relief/escape, not the obvious thing).
- **Swap the routine, hold cue + reward constant** — the most reliable way to change a habit, not sheer suppression.
- **To build a habit, engineer all three:** a stable cue (→ [habit stacking](habit-stacking-and-temptation-bundling.md)), low-friction routine (→ [make it easy](make-it-easy-context-design.md)), and an immediate reward.
