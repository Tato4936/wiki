# Make it easy: context design

> **TL;DR.** Most of daily behavior is **cued by context, not chosen by willpower** — so the highest-leverage move is to *redesign the environment*: cut the friction (steps, distance, decisions) for the behavior you want, and add friction to the one you don't. People with "good self-control" mostly aren't grinding harder; they've arranged their lives so the right thing is the easy default.

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** high · **Updated:** 2026-06-08

## What it is

Wendy Wood's research reframes habits as **context–behavior associations**. In diary studies, roughly **43% of daily actions** were performed habitually — same context, little conscious thought (Wood, Quinn & Kashy, 2002). Because the *context* is the trigger, motivation and intentions predict habitual behavior poorly; the environment predicts it well.

Two consequences:

- **Friction is the lever.** A few seconds or steps of added/removed effort reliably shifts behavior — far more than resolve does. Make the good option the path of least resistance and the bad option annoying to reach.
- **Self-control is mostly habit, not grit.** Galla & Duckworth (2015) found people high in trait self-control succeed largely *because they have better habits* and face fewer temptations — they've engineered exposure, not just resisted it.

A useful corollary is **habit discontinuity**: when context changes (a move, new job, travel), old cues vanish and habits become briefly malleable — the best window to install new ones.

## How to apply

- **Reduce friction for the good habit:** lay out gym clothes the night before; keep the guitar on a stand, the fruit on the counter.
- **Add friction to the bad one:** log out, delete the app, unplug the TV, leave the [phone in another room](../focus/distraction-and-dopamine.md).
- **Exploit transitions:** moving or changing routines? Deliberately design the new defaults *before* new bad habits fill the vacuum.
