# Stepped-up basis

> **TL;DR.** When you inherit an asset, its cost basis resets to market value at the owner's death — so all
> the appreciation during their lifetime is **never income-taxed**. It's the quiet linchpin that makes
> ["buy, borrow, die"](buy-borrow-die.md) work.

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** high · **Updated:** 2026-06-04

## What it is

Capital-gains tax is charged on the gain above your "basis" (usually what you paid). Under US law
(IRC §1014), when you **inherit** an asset, your basis is stepped up to its **fair market value on the date
of death** — not what the deceased originally paid.

Example: grandma bought stock for $1; it's worth $100 when she dies. Her heir's basis becomes **$100**. If
the heir sells at $100, the taxable gain is **$0** — the $99 of lifetime appreciation vanishes for
income-tax purposes, forever.

That's why the "die" in buy-borrow-die is so powerful: you can hold assets with huge unrealized gains,
borrow against them tax-free while alive, and the gains are **wiped clean** at death rather than taxed.

!!! note "Caveats"
    The **estate tax** can still hit large estates (a separate tax from income tax). The step-up is a
    long-standing **policy-debate target** — proposals to repeal it (carryover basis, or taxing gains at
    death) recur. And it's **US law specifically**; many countries handle inherited-asset gains differently.

## How to apply

- A planning implication even for ordinary families: **highly-appreciated assets can be worth holding
  *until death*** rather than selling, because heirs may inherit the gain tax-free — whereas **gifting** the
  same asset during life carries over the low basis (no step-up).
- Genuinely consequential for inheritance decisions; worth a real advisor when the amounts are large.
