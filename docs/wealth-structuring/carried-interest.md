# Carried interest

> **TL;DR.** Private-equity, VC, and hedge-fund managers take ~20% of the fund's profits ("carry") as their
> pay — and it's taxed as **long-term capital gains** (low rate) rather than **ordinary income**, even
> though it's compensation for managing *other people's* money. The famous "carried interest loophole."

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** high · **Updated:** 2026-06-04

## What it is

A fund manager typically charges "2 and 20": a 2% management fee (taxed as ordinary income) plus **20% of
the profits** — the **carried interest**. Because that 20% is structured as a share of the fund's
*investment gains*, it's taxed at the preferential **long-term capital-gains rate**, not the higher
ordinary-income rate that would hit a salaried employee's bonus.

The critique: it's effectively a **performance fee for labor** (the manager often risks little of their own
capital) taxed like an *investor's* return. It's one of the most-debated provisions in US tax — repeatedly
targeted, never fully closed; the 2017 tax law only added a **3-year holding requirement** to qualify.
Still legal.

!!! note "Contested as policy, not as legality"
    Whether carry *should* be taxed as capital gains is a genuine, live policy fight. That it currently *is*
    (subject to the holding-period rule) is settled law. The ✅ here is on the mechanics, not the politics.

## How to apply

- It's the clearest real-world example of the [capital-vs-labor](capital-vs-labor-income.md) game:
  **re-characterizing pay as investment return** to get the lower rate.
- Mostly relevant if you're heading toward fund management or founding; otherwise it's "how the finance
  elite are structured."
