# Caffeine & adenosine

> **TL;DR.** Caffeine doesn't add energy — it **blocks the brain's sleepiness signal (adenosine)**, masking fatigue without removing it. With a **half-life of ~5–6 hours**, an afternoon coffee still has a meaningful dose at bedtime, quietly eroding deep sleep even if you fall asleep fine. The single highest-yield rule: **no caffeine in the second half of your day.**

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** high · **Updated:** 2026-06-08

## What it is

While you're awake, **adenosine** accumulates and builds "sleep pressure" — the drive to sleep — in Borbély's two-process model (homeostatic pressure + the circadian clock). **Caffeine is an adenosine-receptor antagonist:** it occupies the receptors so the pressure can't register. The pressure is still there; you've borrowed alertness against it, and it lands later (the "afternoon crash" when caffeine clears and accumulated adenosine floods back).

The pharmacokinetics are the actionable part. Caffeine's half-life is roughly **5–6 hours** (quarter-life ~10–12h), and varies with genetics (the **CYP1A2** liver enzyme), pregnancy, and other drugs. So a 3pm coffee can leave ~25% circulating near midnight — enough to **reduce deep/slow-wave sleep and sleep efficiency**, often without you noticing you slept worse.

## How to apply

- **Cut off caffeine ~8–10 hours before bed** (for many people, none after about 2pm).
- **Watch hidden sources** — tea, dark chocolate, pre-workout, some sodas and meds.
- **Break the loop:** poor sleep → more caffeine → worse sleep. Fix sleep first, and the caffeine need usually falls.
- ⚠️ The popular tip to **delay your first coffee ~90 min after waking** (let overnight adenosine clear, blunt the crash) is plausible but **low-evidence** — harmless to try, don't treat as established.
