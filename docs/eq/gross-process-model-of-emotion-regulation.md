# Gross's process model of emotion regulation

> **TL;DR.** Emotions unfold in a sequence — situation → attention → appraisal → response — and you
> can intervene at any stage. Earlier interventions are generally cheaper and more effective than late
> ones.

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** high · **Updated:** 2026-06-03

## What it is

James Gross's process model organizes emotion regulation by *where in the emotion-generation timeline*
you act. Five families of strategies, in order:

Situation selection
:   Choosing which situations to enter or avoid (e.g. not scheduling a hard talk when exhausted).

Situation modification
:   Changing the situation you're in (e.g. directly addressing a tension).

Attentional deployment
:   Redirecting attention — distraction, or conversely, focusing.

Cognitive change (appraisal)
:   Reframing what the situation *means* — the lever behind [reappraisal](reappraisal-vs-suppression.md).

Response modulation
:   Acting on the emotion once it has arisen — suppressing expression, exercise, breathing.

The model's central, well-supported insight: **antecedent-focused** strategies (the first four, acting
before the emotion is in full swing) tend to outperform **response-focused** strategies (the last one).
Reframing a situation early is cheaper than wrestling a full-blown emotion down.

## How to apply

- When a feeling is hard to manage, walk *backwards* up the timeline: could you have selected,
  modified, or re-attended earlier? That's where the cheap wins are.
- Build regulation into **situation selection** — most of the gain is in what you walk into, not in
  white-knuckling the aftermath.
- Save response modulation (suppression) for when earlier stages aren't available; it's the costliest
  lever — see [reappraisal vs. suppression](reappraisal-vs-suppression.md).
