# Emotion vocabulary (granularity lexicon)

## Why a bigger emotion vocabulary

**Emotional granularity** (also called *emotion differentiation*) is the ability to construct and label your emotional experiences with precision — to distinguish *anxious* from *dreadful* from *uneasy*, rather than lumping them all under "bad." Lisa Feldman Barrett, who introduced the construct, frames it as a difference in the *resolution* of emotional experience: low-granularity individuals experience broad, undifferentiated affect, while high-granularity individuals carve that affect into many specific, situation-appropriate states. Under her **theory of constructed emotion**, emotions are not pre-packaged reactions waiting to be triggered; the brain *constructs* them in the moment by combining raw bodily affect (valence and arousal) with concepts learned from language and culture. Your emotion vocabulary is therefore not a label applied after the fact — it is part of the construction kit. A larger, more precise vocabulary gives the brain finer-grained concepts to predict with, so a richer lexicon literally changes what you are able to feel.

This matters because **a precise label is actionable**. Barrett et al. (2001) found that people higher in negative-emotion differentiation regulate more effectively — they draw on a wider, more tailored range of coping strategies, because "I'm resentful that I was overlooked" points to a different response than "I'm lonely" or "I'm exhausted," whereas "I feel bad" points nowhere. Higher granularity is associated with less emotional reactivity, less drinking to cope, and better mental-health outcomes. The mechanism is reinforced by **affect labelling**: Lieberman et al. (2007) showed that simply putting feelings into words dampens amygdala response and recruits the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex — naming an emotion is itself a down-regulation. Granularity is the skill that makes that naming accurate, so building a richer emotion vocabulary is a direct, trainable lever on regulation and wellbeing.

## The map: valence × arousal

A few organising maps make the lexicon navigable. Use them as scaffolding, not doctrine — they overlap and disagree, which is the point.

- **Russell's circumplex (valence × arousal).** The base coordinate system: every feeling sits somewhere on two axes — pleasant↔unpleasant (valence) and activated↔deactivated (arousal). *Anxious* is unpleasant + high-arousal; *content* is pleasant + low-arousal; *bored* is unpleasant + low-arousal. Locating a vague feeling on these two dials is often the fastest first cut.
- **Emotion families.** Treat broad terms as *families* with members of varying intensity and flavour: the anger family runs from *irritated* through *frustrated* to *enraged*; the fear family from *uneasy* to *terrified*. Granularity is largely the move from naming the family to naming the member.
- **Plutchik's wheel (primaries + blends).** Eight primaries (joy, trust, fear, surprise, sadness, disgust, anger, anticipation) arranged as opposites, each varying by intensity, with *dyads* formed by blending neighbours (e.g. joy + trust = love; fear + surprise = awe). Good for seeing emotions as combinations.
- **Cowen & Keltner's 27 categories.** A data-driven map showing reported emotion is better described by ~27 categories (e.g. admiration, awe, anxiety, nostalgia, satisfaction, craving) linked by *smooth gradients* rather than a handful of discrete basics — evidence that fine distinctions are real, not pedantic.
- **Willcox "feelings wheel."** A practical three-ring tool: a small core ring (mad, sad, scared, joyful, powerful, peaceful) expanding outward into progressively more specific words. The everyday device for pushing from a core term to a precise one. Brené Brown's *Atlas of the Heart* maps 87 such terms in the same spirit.

## How to use this to build granularity

- **Ban "bad" and "good."** When you catch "I feel bad/good/fine/stressed/off," treat it as a prompt, not an answer. Force one more step to a specific word: *disappointed? overwhelmed? resentful? wistful?*
- **Push to a more precise neighbour.** Whatever word you land on first, ask "is there a sharper one?" *Sad → grieving, dejected, wistful, hollow.* *Anxious → apprehensive, dreading, on-edge, restless.* Use the Willcox wheel or a 27-category list as a lookup until the moves are automatic.
- **Distinguish near-neighbours deliberately.** Pick two adjacent words and articulate the difference for yourself: guilt (*I did a bad thing*) vs shame (*I am bad*); envy (*I want what you have*) vs jealousy (*I fear losing what's mine*); lonely vs bored; nervous vs excited. The contrasts are where granularity is built.
- **Read the body and the action tendency.** Locate the feeling on Russell's two dials first — pleasant or unpleasant, high or low energy — then check sensations (tight chest, hot face, heavy limbs) and the urge it carries (withdraw, approach, attack, freeze). Different urges usually mean different emotions.
- **Label in the moment, in words.** Name it as it happens ("right now I'm feeling X") rather than reconstructing later — that is the affect-labelling effect doing its regulatory work. A quick written note or one phrase to a trusted person counts.
- **Learn a few new words a week.** Deliberately add vocabulary — borrow from *The Book of Human Emotions* (e.g. *l'appel du vide*, *torschlusspanik*) or *Atlas of the Heart* — and use a new term once. The concept has to exist before you can feel with it precisely.
- **Mind the positive and low-arousal blind spot.** Most people are far more granular about high-arousal negatives than about calm or pleasant states. Practise distinguishing *content, serene, satisfied, grateful, tender, relieved, at-ease* — and notice quiet low-energy feelings (flat, drained, listless) that "fine" usually hides.
