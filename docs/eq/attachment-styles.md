# Attachment styles

> **TL;DR.** How people behave in close relationships tends to follow one of a few **attachment styles** —
> secure, anxious, avoidant — formed early but carried into adult friendships and romance. Knowing your
> own and reading others' explains a lot of relational friction, and how to bridge it.

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** high · **Updated:** 2026-06-04

## What it is

Attachment theory (Bowlby & Ainsworth) began with infants and caregivers; Hazan & Shaver showed the same
patterns shape **adult** close relationships. A common four-style map (Bartholomew & Horowitz) sits on two
axes — anxiety (fear of abandonment) and avoidance (discomfort with closeness):

Secure
:   Comfortable with intimacy *and* independence; communicates needs directly. Roughly half of people.

Anxious (preoccupied)
:   Craves closeness, fears abandonment, needs reassurance; can read distance as rejection.

Avoidant (dismissing)
:   Values independence, uncomfortable with too much closeness or emotional demand; withdraws under
    pressure. (This is the pattern the [Partner archetypes](../relationships/partner-archetypes.md) note's
    "steady professional" was gesturing at.)

Fearful (disorganized)
:   Wants closeness but fears it; push–pull.

Styles are **tendencies, not boxes** — they shift across partners and over time, and people can move
toward security ("earned secure").

## How to apply

- **Know your own pattern** — it explains your default reactions (chasing when anxious, withdrawing when
  avoidant) so you can name them instead of just acting them out.
- **Read the other person's.** With an avoidant, pursuing harder backfires — give space and steady,
  low-pressure consistency. With an anxious person, **proactive reassurance** heads off the spiral.
- The pairing to watch is **anxious + avoidant**: one pursues, one retreats, each triggering the other.
  Naming the dynamic out loud is the first repair.
