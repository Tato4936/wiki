# Mayer–Salovey ability model

> **TL;DR.** Emotional intelligence is best defined as a set of *abilities* — perceiving, using,
> understanding, and managing emotions — not a bundle of personality traits. This "four-branch" model
> is the scientifically dominant definition of EI.

**Provenance:** `cited` · **Confidence:** high · **Updated:** 2026-06-03

## What it is

Salovey and Mayer introduced "emotional intelligence" in 1990 and refined it into a **four-branch
ability model** in 1997. EI is treated as a genuine intelligence — a capacity to reason *about* and
*with* emotions — measured by performance, like IQ, rather than by self-report.

The four branches, from most basic to most complex:

Perceiving emotion
:   Identifying emotions in faces, voices, and oneself.

Using emotion (facilitating thought)
:   Harnessing emotions to direct attention and aid reasoning or creativity.

Understanding emotion
:   Knowing how emotions combine, progress, and what causes them — emotional "grammar."

Managing emotion
:   Regulating one's own and others' emotions to reach goals.

This is the **ability** tradition, and it marks the key fork in the EI field: it contrasts with the
**mixed** and **trait** models (e.g. Goleman, Bar-On), which fold in motivation, well-being, and
personality. The ability model has the strongest construct validity; mixed models are popular but
psychometrically muddier because they overlap heavily with existing personality measures.

## How to apply

- Treat EI as a **trainable skill**, not a fixed trait — each branch can be practiced.
- The branches are a diagnostic ladder: weak emotion *management* often traces back to weak
  *perception* or *understanding* upstream. Fix the lower rung first.
- Be skeptical of anything selling "EQ" as a personality quiz — that's the mixed/trait tradition, not
  this one.
